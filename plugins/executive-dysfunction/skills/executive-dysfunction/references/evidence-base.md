# Evidence base

## Purpose and limits

This reference supports an AI assistant workflow. It does not convert the assistant into a clinician or make each workflow technique a treatment.

The operational skill adapts common components of structured psychological support into low-risk assistance: externalising plans, making actions observable, reducing environmental friction, and checking progress. It does not reproduce a treatment manual.

## Evidence tiers

### 1. Guideline-backed

Use these as the strongest design constraints.

- **Shared decisions and personal goals:** NICE states that people have the right to participate in care decisions. The Australian guideline recommends person-centred multimodal support that reflects preferences, strengths, needs, and goals. [1][2]
- **Tailored, manageable information:** the Australian guideline recommends plain, non-judgmental information in smaller chunks, written reinforcement, multimodal presentation, and checks for understanding. [6]
- **Environmental modifications:** NICE describes reducing distractions, modifying noise or lighting, using shorter focus periods with movement breaks, and reinforcing verbal requests with written instructions. Modifications must fit the individual. [1]
- **Structured psychological support:** NICE recommends a structured supportive psychological intervention for adults when non-pharmacological treatment is indicated; this may include CBT elements. The Australian guideline supports ADHD-specific cognitive-behavioural interventions and environmental modifications. [1][2][6]

**Agent translation:** collaborate, externalise information, reduce cognitive load, modify the task environment, and preserve user choice.

### 2. Trial-supported

Use these as intervention components, not guarantees for an individual.

- Safren et al. tested a 12-session CBT program against relaxation plus educational support in 86 medication-treated adults with persistent ADHD symptoms. CBT produced greater symptom improvement, maintained at follow-up. [4]
- Solanto et al. tested a 12-week group metacognitive intervention focused on time management, organisation, and planning against supportive psychotherapy in 88 adults. The intervention produced greater improvement on several symptom ratings. [7]
- Knouse et al. synthesised 32 adult ADHD CBT studies, with up to 896 participants. Effects were smaller against active controls than from pre- to post-treatment, and outcomes were heterogeneous. [5]
- Cochrane found low-quality evidence that CBT may improve core ADHD symptoms in adults in the short term and noted uncertainty about longer-term effects. [3]
- A later systematic review found behavioural therapy most consistently supported for adult inattention. It did not find control-group effects for cognitive training, neurofeedback, or psychoeducation in the included trials. [8]

**Agent translation:** define completion, break work into observable steps, schedule actions, externalise organisation, review results, and adjust the system.

### 3. Indirect or preference-based

Offer these as reversible experiments. Do not describe them as established ADHD treatments.

- **Body doubling:** widely used in ADHD communities, but there is limited direct evidence that isolates body doubling as an intervention. Offer it only when the user prefers social presence or accountability.
- **ADHD coaching:** there is preliminary or suggestive evidence, mostly from uncontrolled, self-reported, or observational work. Efficacy and safety remain uncertain, and randomized trials are needed. An AI can borrow collaborative goal-setting patterns without claiming to provide credentialed coaching. [10][11]
- **Implementation intentions, focus timers, immediate rewards, and visual task boards:** these draw on broader behavioural and self-regulation principles. ADHD-specific effects vary, so test one method against the user's experience.
- **Named productivity systems:** Pomodoro, priority matrices, and similar systems are interfaces, not treatments. Use them only when they reduce rather than add friction.
- **Reminders and structured support from other people:** qualitative evidence describes these as useful coping supports, but it does not establish comparative treatment effects. [9]

## Design implications

1. Select one bottleneck before selecting a strategy.
2. Reduce the number of decisions and keep one current action visible.
3. Prefer an external support over a demand for memory or motivation.
4. Treat response as individual evidence: retain helpful supports and replace unhelpful ones.
5. Never use a support technique to override consent, clinical care, rest, or safety.

## Evidence cautions

- Clinical guideline recommendations combine research evidence, professional expertise, feasibility, and lived experience. Recommendation strength varies.
- CBT trials evaluate multi-component clinician-delivered programs. They do not prove that one isolated prompt or planning technique has the same effect.
- Group averages do not predict an individual's response.
- Evidence for adults does not automatically generalise to children.
- This review is bounded, not a living systematic review. Recheck primary guidance before making clinical or policy claims.
